Bloc 11 Climbing Gym · Johannesburg · 2025

Bloc 11 Fourways

Sole designer. Site selection to wall completion.

An empty retail shell to a 900 m² climbing gym in under three months.

01AUse

Floor plan, main climbing hall

01A

Use

Bouldering is climbing without ropes, on walls low enough to jump off onto foam matting.

The walls serve two groups with different needs. Climbers choose a gym on variety, so the design needed as many angles and configurations as the space allowed. Route setters need maximum unobstructed surface for volumes and screw-on holds, since that determines the quality of routes they can set, plus simple open structure behind the wall so damaged T-nuts are easy to reach.

The layout leaves trolley paths through the matting, connecting storage at the back of the gym to the floor.

01B

Form

A bouldering gym mimics the boulders and caves you find outside. The objective is the balance between face variance and simplicity.

I hand-measured the retail shell and rebuilt it in CAD, then ideated at full scale in VR through Gravity Sketch, sketching structural forms freely and verifying them against the accurate model of the space. Once the managing director and I agreed on the rough shapes, I moved into CAD to model dimensionally accurate surfaces and build the structure off those.

01C

Structure

Two sections of heavy steel tubing span the concrete pillars and fix to the brick walls, forming the foundation. Plywood spines mount off them on custom laser-cut plates that allow micro-adjustment during assembly.

That answered the previous build, where panels hung off steel arms with no fixed plane to index from, compounding errors along a long run of wall.

Lipped top and bottom edges do three jobs: alignment guides, lateral stability, and a splinter-free hold where climbers grab over the top.

The system was intentionally light, so once a section stood we could see where reinforcement was actually needed and add it there. Less material, faster assembly.

01D

Build

I produced every production file on the job: floor plans, concept designs, CAD models, CNC cutting files and assembly diagrams, plus supplier and material sourcing. I trained the CNC operator before the venue was settled.

The managing director ran construction alongside me from a traditional woodworking background while I set the assembly method and corrected on site, continuing to cut CNC files for batches shipped from their Cape Town workshop.

When the budget tightened, I changed the spines from solid sheets with access holes to interlocking strips reinforced with timber. Less material, same system.

Turnkey Interiors (Pty) Ltd · Johannesburg · 2026

The Student Den

Sole designer. Research to producible design.

Frees 47.5% of the floor in a 6 m² room while adding nearly 2,000 litres of storage.

02AUse

Render of socialising in the room

02A

Use

South African student accommodation is built to a minimum of 8 m² for a single room, and developers are already testing 6 m². Into that space go sleeping, studying, storage, eating and hosting, which is why the bed ends up being the chair, the desk and the table.

The buyer and the user are different people with different problems. Students rated guest seating their single worst frustration and storage second. Developers do not buy student comfort. They buy reduced risk, controlled cost and easier maintenance, inside an FF&E envelope of roughly R30,000 per bed. The design had to satisfy both, because the student has no say in what gets installed.

02B

Form

The unit is a single integrated system rather than a set of separate pieces: a steel bed frame, a hanging closet, a second closet elevated off the bed frame, a desk and shelf run bridging between them, and four under-bed boxes whose lipped lids double as seating or a coffee table.

Pulling everything into one run along the wall frees 2,033 x 1,400 mm of open floor, about 2.85 m² of a 6 m² room. Total storage comes to roughly 1,073 litres inside the unit and another 894 above and behind it.

Vertical space does the work. Most student rooms use only the lower 1.5 m of wall, so the second closet sits above the bed and the desk tucks under it rather than claiming its own footprint.

02C

Function

Rather than a transformation mechanism, function is integrated into the fittings: a recessed LED strip for work and reading with an under-desk switch, an electrical outlet built into the desk, a full-length mirror recessed into the cupboard door, and a cupboard handle with a hole sized for the student's own padlock rather than a supplied lock.

The bed frame's bolting positions are all on the sides, because a frame running the width of a 2 m room cannot be reached at the ends once it's in position. A welded mattress slide stopper holds both 1880 and 2000 mm mattresses without a head lip, so developers can buy mattresses off the shelf.

02D

Build

Every cabinet is 16 mm sheet material assembled with Lamello Cabineo 12 flat-pack connectors, so the unit ships flat and goes together on site with hand tools. 16 mm is a standard cabinetmaking thickness, which means the specified melamine can be swapped for another substrate without redesign when a project calls for it.

Steel is confined to where it earns its place: the bed frame, the elevated closet's support bracket, and the sheet-steel handles and padlock brackets. Everything else is panel work producible on a standard CNC router.

Finish is the variable. Melamine faces and powder-coated metal let a developer set colour per building without touching a structural component.

The unit was designed with production in mind, dimensioned against standard sheet sizes and detailed to a documented build. No physical prototype was produced.

Unity Hands

A giant public seat built to get a district voting, unveiled by the mayor.

03AUse

Co-creation sessions in Escamp

03A

Use

Turnout in The Hague’s 2022 municipal election was 43%, and Escamp, the city’s largest district, was among the lowest of eight. The people least represented were the least likely to vote: immigrant women, who told us they felt divided, afraid and disconnected from civic processes.

The response was citywide: eight landmark chairs, one per district, referencing the 45 seats on the city council. Escamp got ours.

So the chair could not look like it came from the government. Street conversations kept returning to “we don’t do politics” the moment the gemeente was mentioned, so the piece had to read as politically neutral and community-owned rather than institutional. It carries voting information in six languages on panels built into the structure.

03B

Form

Two cupped hands, three metres across, with STRONGER TOGETHER cut through the fingers. The letters are stained in different shades of skin tone and the fingernails vary in shape and colour, so the diversity of the district is in the object rather than described on it.

Form followed safety as much as symbolism. No gap exceeds 20 mm, there are no enclosed spaces a person could hide in, and the tallest climbable surface is capped at 600 mm so the piece needs no shock-absorbent surfacing. Part of the seat sits at 400 to 450 mm to stay reachable for people of all physical abilities.

03C

Function

The brief called for a piece that could travel the district, so the chair was designed to come apart: bolted connections into threaded inserts, no glue in the main assembly, and a two-hour assembly and disassembly cycle documented in the usage booklet alongside the ballast loading procedure.

It also had to stand unattended in a public square. 300 kg of internal ballast, inaccessible from outside, puts the force needed to shift it above 200 kg and holds it against wind load, and no joint can be opened without tools.

By manufacture the municipality had settled on a single location for the campaign, and the builder fixed the connections rather than bolting them.

03D

Build

12 mm Okoume plywood fingers on a 15 mm Okoume base, CNC cut flat and mechanically fixed. No glue in the main assembly, so every material can be separated at end of life.

I produced the technical drawings, every cutting list and DXF, and prepared the laser-cutting files for the lettering in Rhino, coordinating two manufacturers: one cutting the main structure, one cutting the lettering panels. I specified the internal timber structure, the ballast method and the weight limits. I did the costing and supplier sourcing, and negotiated the budget with the municipality against what the design actually required.

I also wrote the full usage guidelines booklet: assembly and disassembly sequence, ballast loading, relocation and upkeep. Read the usage guidelines booklet ↗

Everything is fire-resistant marine plywood with natural stains and water-based coatings, specified to biodegrade within ten years.

Technology for Health Care, THUAS · The Hague · 2024

FULLER

Concept, CAD and prototype.

A drink dispenser for care homes, built so every wetted part survives a dishwasher at 85°C.

The brief

Redesign the smart drink dispenser so that it seamlessly integrates into elderly care facility environments, and can better address the needs of both caretakers and residents.

04AUse

The previous team's design next to FULLER

04A

Use

The dispenser lets residents in care facilities pour their own drinks at mealtimes, which takes work off staff and gives residents back a small piece of independence. Two groups have to be satisfied at once, and they want different things.

It had to serve eight residents in one sitting, hold 4.5 litres, and stay under 12 kg fully loaded so a caretaker could lift it. Residents also had to move it themselves across a table while seated, which caps the weight before anything else is decided.

The existing prototype served neither group well. Residents got a device that looked juvenile, closer to a toy than to something an adult would choose to use in front of other people. Staff got one they struggled to clean, service and store, with rails that only worked in some rooms.

04B

Form

Package layouts came first, tested and narrowed. Each layout fixes where the containers, the valve assembly, the electronics and the drip tray sit in relation to each other, and the sketches then work the preferred layouts into resolved bodies. The form develops from the internal components outward, which is why the configuration holds steady across the sheets and the differences are in the body.

The target was inconspicuous. A care home is somebody’s home, so the dispenser needed to read as furniture rather than as medical equipment or as a toy. An oak-effect wrap carries the warmth, with a brushed stainless drip tray where the function is visible.

The aesthetic decisions were made against Gestalt principles rather than taste. Continuity gives the body a pyramidal read, which carries an impression of stability. The interactive components are all circular, grouped by similarity so the eye collects them, and circular against the angular body balances the whole.

04C

Function

The device dispenses by gravity, so the only job the electronics have is to open and close a valve. My first design actuated that valve from below. That put the mechanism in the liquid path, widened the lid, made channelling the liquid through the device harder, and made the valve awkward to assemble and dismantle. Moving the actuation out solved all four: the solenoid sits clear of the liquid and stays in place, and the only parts that have to come out are the ones that get wet.

Everything in contact with liquid withstands 85°C, which is dishwasher temperature. Each selection dispenses 200 ml, stopping 2 cm from the rim, through its own tube to prevent cross-contamination. The panel is capped at four buttons and pouring takes no more than five steps.

04D

Build

For the handle assembly I compared three fastening methods: screws and bolts, clips, and joints. Tapered dovetail joints won on strength and simplicity, with additional nubs locking the parts once fully assembled. Making the handles detachable also closed the dead volume between base and upper body, which matters for shipping and for storing the unit between meals.

I prepared every 3D file and all the slicer settings, printed the parts, and assembled the prototype. The buttons use mechanical keyboard switches so the panel actually clicks under a finger, and the handle wraps are printed in flexible TPU to stand in for silicone.

The valve would need further design for manufacture work before it could be injection moulded.

Interactive Maquette

A Delft-blue maquette that teaches DC microgrids by letting visitors load the grid by hand.

05AUse

My storyboard, visitor interaction

05A

Use

The DC Power Laboratory had already built a maquette to explain its research, but it needed someone standing next to it to explain it. The new object had to teach on its own, at conferences and open days, to STEM students who mostly rated their own knowledge of AC and DC at 1 or 2 out of 5.

The lab wanted something robust that one person could set up quickly. Students wanted hands-on, game-like interaction, and a screen for more information. The object had to carry the lesson without a guide and still survive being moved from venue to venue.

05B

Form

We tested a wall layout against a hexagonal board by bodystorming and an in-person survey. Users preferred the hexagon at around 1 m, because it reads from every side and lets several people use it at once. Dot voting settled a symmetrical layout for the houses.

The houses are Delft-style, with the Oude Kerk at the centre, so the grid reads as a Dutch town rather than an abstract diagram. Delft blue on white was chosen because it is familiar to a Dutch audience and quick to paint: a white coat with blue detail on top.

05C

Function

The main input is a wheel with a crank, chosen through Wizard of Oz testing of four physical inputs. The wheel turns a rotary encoder, and an Arduino drives an LED strip that rises like a bar graph as the load on the grid increases. Buttons on the board light up a grid component and bring up its information on a screen through Processing. Gabriel Farrar-Hockley built the wheel and the LED system.

The client could not supply the electronics for a full grid, so we split the system into three sections that work separately, built from the equipment we had.

05D

Build

I modelled the Oude Kerk and the board, and merged the team’s house models into one Fusion assembly. The church was heavy enough to slow the assembly down, which taught me to constrain large assemblies properly.

I ran the print tests and set the print settings. Changing the infill pattern and amount cut print time by up to 60% and material cost by about 66%. The tests also showed that open windows lit better and that the house details printed without supports. Printing started a week early across several locations, partly at our own cost, after the client cut the budget. Maciej Michalski managed the print jobs and made the final renders, and the four of us painted and assembled the model.

At the expo, the electronics were only partly working. Visitors found the wheel intuitive, though it needs more resistance, and the Delft aesthetic landed well with Dutch visitors. Some criticised the use of plastic for a sustainability project.

06AUse

Playtesting with a blindfolded runner

06A

Use

One player is blindfolded and does all the moving. The other is the guide, three steps outside the play area, and can only talk. Neither can win alone, which is the educational part the brief asked for: communication, coordination and cooperation.

We narrowed the brief’s age range of 3 to 10 to 5 and up, for safety and so children could play without an adult.

Parents in our survey wanted a game their children could play with or without supervision, stored in a box. Educators wanted less involvement and preferred a cloth bag. Cloth was expensive and the brief required the packaging to double as storage, so it ships in a cardboard box.

06B

Form

From week 6 to 10 each of us developed concepts separately. Mine was Mole Hunt, with mole-shaped objectives and snake and bear-trap obstacles, and it was the one the team took into final development. There we dropped the mole theme so the game would read as more general, and kept the roles Mole Hunt had set up: a guide, a blind runner, ordered objectives and obstacles that penalise contact.

Each family of parts was then judged against the requirements rather than on preference: Harris profiles for the trophies and the dice graphics, weighted objectives for the blindfolds and the packaging, a C-Box for the obstacles.

The brief excluded pink to keep the toy gender-neutral and ruled out hyper-stimulating colours. The palette is orange, yellow and green, and each team plays as a colour, green or yellow, carried through its blindfold and trophies. The dice faces are pictographic, so every instruction reads without text.

06C

Function

Each turn the guide rolls a jumbo dice that sets a condition on the runner’s next move, such as walk backwards or guide can’t talk. The runner takes two steps on the guide’s instructions and collects their trophies in numbered order.

Each floor piece stands on an interlocking foot base. On its own the joint would come apart, so I tied the parts together with natural rubber elastic. Each piece stays one part, standing in play or folded flat in the box, so nothing gets lost and setting up means unfolding rather than assembling. The T-trap does the opposite: three loose pieces that fall when bumped, so a touch the runner can’t see is obvious to everyone else. Bump one and you spin three times. Hit a rope trap and the other team puts one of your trophies back in the field.

The blindfold tightens with a wooden slip ring. It isn’t a new mechanism, but it is cheap, biodegradable and reliable, and the client liked it. Players set the difficulty by how close together they lay the traps.

06D

Build

I modelled every part in Fusion 360 and had the ply parts laser cut. The geometry was kept open enough for a corner-rounding router bit to reach every edge, and narrow sections were designed out after the snake’s tongue on the first obstacle was flagged as too fragile. I made the two jumbo dice by hand from pine. The box was designed in 3D, then I drew the flat pattern out by hand and cut it myself. The team helped paint it and make the wrap.

The first costing came to €32.99 a unit varnished and €22.43 unvarnished, against an €8 ceiling. I brought it to €7.91 by cutting the number of pieces, reducing material thickness and simplifying the printed graphics. The unit cost comes from a quote by a manufacturer in China, not an estimate.
